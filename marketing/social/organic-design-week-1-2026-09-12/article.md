# Minimal is a design choice, not a score

A browser can look beautifully quiet in a screenshot and still make an ordinary task awkward. Another can look busy and put the control you need exactly where you expect it.

That is why counting buttons is a poor substitute for trying the work.

When you are deciding whether to change browsers, start with one task you already understand. Review a design. Join a routine call. Open the files you use to run your business. Choose something small enough to try without moving your whole working life into a new tool.

Then ask three questions.

## Can I find what I need?

A hidden control has not stopped existing. You still need a way to reach it, and you need to know where to look. A keyboard shortcut might be wonderful for someone who remembers it. The same action may need an obvious label for someone using the browser for the first time.

Neither person is using the tool incorrectly. They are asking different things of its design.

Notice the moment you hesitate. Were you searching for a familiar word? Looking in the place where another app puts the command? Waiting for an icon to explain itself? Those details are more useful than simply saying an interface feels clean or cluttered.

## Can I do the ordinary thing?

Try the details that rarely appear in a launch image: signing in, opening the relevant file, finding a setting, moving between the pages your task needs.

You do not need an exhaustive test suite. You need an honest account of whether this tool fits your task. Keep your current browser available while you try the alternative. A trial should leave room to decide against switching.

The result may be mixed. You may prefer one interaction and miss another. That is a useful answer, too.

## Can I recover?

Make a small, harmless mistake. Open the wrong page. Choose a setting you can safely reverse. Look for the way back.

Do not test recovery by closing unsaved work. The point is to understand the interface, not to put anything important at risk.

Recovery belongs in a design conversation because trying something unfamiliar involves uncertainty. A reassuring route back can matter more than another polished screenshot.

## Tell us the moment, not just the verdict

This is the kind of conversation we want around Blanc. Which action was easy? Which one made you stop? What would prevent you from switching, even if you liked the look?

A specific example gives us something to discuss. It may also help another reader recognize what they need from their own browser.
